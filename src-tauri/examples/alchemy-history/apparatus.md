Alchemy was a laboratory practice before it was anything else, and its furnaces, stills, and vessels, many of them devised in Roman Egypt, are the direct ancestors of the equipment in a modern chemistry bench.

## Heat

Every operation depended on controlled heat, and alchemists graded it carefully. The gentlest was the dung bath: a vessel buried in fermenting horse manure, which held a mild, steady warmth for weeks and was used for digestion and putrefaction. Next came the water bath, the balneum Mariae or bain-marie, named for Maria the Jewess and still in use under that name; it could not exceed the boiling point of water and so protected delicate distillations. Sand and ash baths gave hotter, more even heat than a naked flame. Direct fire, controlled by bellows, served for calcination and melting.

The furnace built for long work was the athanor, from the Arabic al-tannur, an oven. It was a tower of charcoal feeding a fire chamber by gravity, so that a single charge burned for days without tending. The philosophical egg, a sealed glass flask holding the matter of the Great Work, sat in the athanor for weeks or months while its contents changed color. Furnaces with movable covers, later called muffles, let a crucible be heated without contact with fuel or flame, which mattered for assaying.

## Distillation

The still is the alchemist's signature instrument, and it took its shape in Alexandria. The base was a cucurbit, a gourd-shaped flask holding the liquid. On top sat the ambix, a cap in which the vapor condensed, with a spout carrying the condensate to a receiver. Arabic writers called the cap al-anbiq, and Latin turned that into alembic, which came to mean the whole apparatus. Maria's tribikos had three spouts feeding three receivers at once. Cooling was by air or by wet cloths on the head until water-jacketed condensers appeared in the late Middle Ages.

The retort, a single flask with a long neck bent downward, combined boiler and head and was easier to seal. Distillation repeated many times on the same liquid was cohobation, and it was the standard way to concentrate spirits. Aqua vitae, distilled wine, was being made in Italy by the twelfth century, and alchemists were the first to describe strong alcohol, nitric acid, sulfuric acid from vitriol, and hydrochloric acid from salt, all products of distillation.

## Sublimation and reflux

Sublimation, heating a solid so that it vaporizes and recondenses as a solid without passing through a liquid, was central because so many alchemical materials, mercury, sulfur, arsenic sulfides, and sal ammoniac, sublime readily. The vessel for it was the aludel, a series of pear-shaped pots stacked over a fire so the vapor rose through them and deposited on the cooler upper walls. Mercuric chloride, "corrosive sublimate," takes its name from the process.

The kerotakis, another of Maria's devices, was a closed vessel with a metal plate or palette near the top. Thin leaves of copper, lead, or silver lay on the plate while sulfur or mercury heated below sent up vapor that attacked the metal and condensed back down to be vaporized again. It is the earliest form of reflux apparatus, and the changes it produced in the metals' color and texture were the observations behind much of the early theory. The pelican, a flask with two side arms curving back into its own body, did the same for liquids, circulating them for days.

## Vessels and seals

Vessels were glass, glazed earthenware, or metal depending on the operation. Venetian glass was prized, but ordinary green glass served. Crucibles for melting were clay; the cupel for assaying was pressed from bone ash, which absorbs lead oxide while leaving gold and silver as a bead. Joints were sealed with lute, a paste of clay, hair, and dung or flour, and the "lute of wisdom" was a recipe alchemists guarded. For the sealed egg the glass neck was softened and fused shut, the original hermetic seal.

## Al-Razi's list and the archaeological record

The Book of Secrets of al-Razi, from around 900, includes an inventory of what a laboratory needs: beakers, flasks, phials, cauldrons, alembics, aludels, funnels, crucibles, sand baths, water baths, ovens, bellows, tongs, shears, files, mortars, and molds. It reads like a supply order, and Latin alchemists copied it as one.

Physical evidence confirms the picture. Excavations from the 1980s at Oberstockstall in Lower Austria uncovered a sixteenth-century laboratory with hundreds of crucibles, cupels, distillation vessels, and glassware, apparently used by a parish priest for assaying and alchemy. Tycho Brahe built an alchemical laboratory into the basement of his observatory on the island of Hven in the 1580s. Paintings by Pieter Bruegel the Elder, David Teniers the Younger, and their followers, whatever their satirical intent, show the same furnaces, retorts, and bellows that survive in the ground.

## Method

What the apparatus does not show is the method that governed its use: weigh before and after, repeat an operation until the product no longer changes, keep the vessel sealed so nothing escapes, and record the colors. Those habits, carried by alchemists for over a millennium, are the part of the practice that chemistry kept without renaming.
