China and India developed alchemical traditions of their own, both older than the Latin tradition and both aimed less at gold than at a perfected, long-lived body.

## China: elixirs and emperors

Chinese alchemy grew out of the search for immortality. The First Emperor, Qin Shi Huang, sent expeditions to find the islands of the immortals in the third century BCE, and the historian Sima Qian records that in 133 BCE a practitioner named Li Shaojun advised Emperor Wu of Han to transmute cinnabar into gold and eat from vessels made of it in order to prolong his life. The pairing is characteristic. Gold interested Chinese alchemists as the incorruptible metal, and the point of making it was to make the body incorruptible too.

The central substance was cinnabar, mercury sulfide, a red mineral that yields liquid mercury when heated and can be turned back into red powder by heating the mercury with sulfur. That reversible cycle, from red to silver and back, became the model of the elixir: a substance that had passed through repeated transformations and returned to itself, called the cyclically transformed elixir, huandan. The word dan, cinnabar, came to mean elixir in general.

## Texts and masters

The foundational text is the Cantong qi, "The Kinship of the Three," attributed to Wei Boyang and traditionally dated to the second century CE. It ties alchemy to the hexagrams of the Book of Changes and to Daoist cosmology, and its language is so cryptic that it was read for centuries as both a furnace manual and a meditation guide. Ge Hong, who lived from 283 to 343, wrote the Baopuzi, "The Master Who Embraces Simplicity," around 320; its inner chapters describe elixir recipes in practical detail, discuss the making of gold, and defend the reality of immortality against skeptics. Tao Hongjing, in the fifth and sixth centuries, organized the Shangqing Daoist tradition and wrote on pharmacology; Sun Simiao, the great Tang physician, left recipes for elixirs alongside his medical works.

The elixirs were dangerous. They were built around mercury, lead, and arsenic, and the historical record includes several Tang emperors whose deaths in the ninth century were attributed to them. A Daoist text of the mid-ninth century warns against heating sulfur, saltpeter, and realgar together because the mixture had burned down houses, which is the earliest known reference to gunpowder; it was found by alchemists looking for something else.

## Internal alchemy

From the Tang period the emphasis shifted from the furnace to the body. Internal alchemy, neidan, kept the vocabulary of external alchemy, waidan, and applied it to breath, essence, and spirit, cultivated through meditation, breathing, and the circulation of energy along channels in the body. The furnace was the lower abdomen, the ingredients were the body's own vital substances, and the golden elixir was a state to be reached rather than a powder to be swallowed. Zhang Boduan's Awakening to Reality of 1075 is the central text, and the Quanzhen school founded in the twelfth century made internal alchemy a monastic discipline. The Secret of the Golden Flower, the text that brought Jung to alchemy, belongs to this tradition.

## India: the science of mercury

In India, the term rasayana appears in the classical Ayurvedic texts of the early centuries CE as a branch of medicine concerned with rejuvenation, using herbal and dietary regimens. From around the seventh or eighth century a distinct discipline formed around mercury, called rasashastra, the science of rasa, a word that means juice, essence, and mercury at once. The alchemist Nagarjuna, a figure separate from the Buddhist philosopher of the same name, though the two were often conflated, is the tradition's legendary founder. The Persian scholar al-Biruni, who traveled in India in the early eleventh century, described its practitioners and their aims.

The major texts date from the tenth to the fourteenth centuries: the Rasarnava, the Rasahridaya, and the Rasaratnasamuccaya, the last a systematic compendium attributed to a Vagbhata. They set out two goals. The lesser was lohavada, the transmutation of metals. The greater was dehavada, the perfection of the body, in which mercury purified by a sequence of eighteen operations was consumed to make the body itself immortal and immune to decay, a condition the texts describe as liberation while still alive.

The framing was religious. Mercury was identified with the seed of the god Shiva and sulfur or mica with the substance of his consort, so that combining them in the laboratory repeated the union that generates the world. The practitioners were often yogis of the Siddha and Nath traditions, and the same texts that describe furnaces describe postures and breath control. Processed mercury and calcined metal ashes, bhasmas, passed into Ayurvedic pharmacy and remain in use there.

## Contacts

The three traditions were not sealed off from one another. Trade routes carried sal ammoniac, cinnabar, and the words for them between China, India, Persia, and the Arab world, and scholars have proposed borrowings in both directions. What is clear is the difference in emphasis. Where Western alchemy made the transmutation of metals its central problem and treated medicine as a corollary, the Chinese and Indian traditions made the transformation of the body central and treated gold as one instrument of it.
