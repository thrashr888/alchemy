Between the eighth and eleventh centuries, scholars writing in Arabic turned Greek alchemy into a systematic discipline with its own theory of metals, its own classification of substances, and its own vocabulary, much of which is still in use.

## Translation and legend

The Abbasid translation movement in Baghdad, running from the mid-eighth century through the tenth, brought Greek and Syriac scientific writing into Arabic, and alchemy came with it. Arabic tradition placed the beginning earlier and gave it a founding story: the Umayyad prince Khalid ibn Yazid, who died around 704, was said to have commissioned translations and learned the art from a Byzantine monk named Morienus. The story is almost certainly a later invention, but it mattered. A dialogue between Khalid and Morienus was the first alchemical text translated into Latin, in 1144.

## The Jabirian corpus

The largest body of Arabic alchemical writing carries the name of Jabir ibn Hayyan, traditionally an eighth-century figure connected to the city of Kufa and to the Shi'ite imam Ja'far al-Sadiq. Several hundred treatises are attributed to him. In the 1940s the historian Paul Kraus argued from their vocabulary and their citations that most of them were written in the late ninth and tenth centuries by more than one author, probably within a Shi'ite intellectual milieu. The debate over the real Jabir continues, but "the Jabirian corpus" is the safer name for the books.

The corpus is ambitious. It works out a theory of balance, the mizan, according to which every substance has a definite proportion of four qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry), and a numerology that assigns those proportions from the letters of the substance's Arabic name. It describes preparing elixirs not only from minerals but from plant and animal matter. It gives procedures for distillation, calcination, and sublimation in detail, and it names reagents such as sal ammoniac that the Greek texts did not know. Its Book of Seventy and Book of Stones circulated in Latin, where the name became Geber.

## Sulfur and mercury

The theory of metals most associated with Arabic alchemy is a refinement of Aristotle. In the Meteorology, metals form underground when two exhalations, one dry and smoky, one moist and vaporous, are trapped and compressed. Arabic writers identified the two with idealized sulfur and mercury: sulfur the principle of combustibility and earthiness, mercury the principle of fusibility and metallic shine. Every metal is a combination of the two. Differences among metals come from the purity of the ingredients and the proportion in which they combine, and gold is what nature produces when both are pure and the balance is exact.

The consequence follows immediately. If lead and gold are the same two things in different states, then an art that could purify and rebalance them could turn one into the other. The sulfur-mercury theory passed into Latin in the twelfth century and remained the standard account of metals in Europe until Paracelsus added salt to it in the sixteenth.

## Al-Razi and the laboratory

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, known in Latin as Rhazes, was born around 865 in Rayy, near modern Tehran, and died there in 925. He was one of the great physicians of the age, but he also wrote on alchemy, and his Book of Secrets (Kitab al-Asrar) is the most matter-of-fact alchemical text of the period. It classifies substances into mineral, vegetable, and animal, and subdivides minerals into spirits (volatile: mercury, sal ammoniac, sulfur, arsenic sulfides), metals, stones, vitriols, boraxes, and salts. It lists the apparatus a laboratory needs, from crucibles and alembics to bellows and tongs, and it describes operations step by step. Al-Razi had little use for allegory. Where the Jabirian books offer number mysticism, he offers a procedure, and later Latin writers borrowed his lists wholesale.

## Ibn Sina's objection

Not everyone was persuaded. The philosopher al-Kindi wrote a refutation of those who claimed to make gold in the ninth century. More influential was Ibn Sina, Avicenna, who lived from 980 to 1037. In the section on minerals in his encyclopedic Book of Healing he accepted the sulfur-mercury account of how metals form but denied that craftsmen could change one metallic species into another. They could tint and imitate; they could not transmute, because the specific difference between metals was not something the art could reach.

This passage was translated into Latin around 1200 by Alfred of Sareshel and, by an accident of manuscript transmission, was attached to the end of Aristotle's Meteorology. For the next three centuries the standard scholastic argument against transmutation was quoted as if Aristotle himself had made it. Defenders of the art, beginning with al-Tughra'i in the early twelfth century, argued back that the art assists nature rather than counterfeiting it.

## The words that stayed

The Latin West took its alchemical vocabulary from Arabic, and English inherited it. Alembic comes from al-anbiq, itself from the Greek ambix. Alkali is from al-qili, the ashes of a saltwort plant. Alcohol began as al-kuhl, the fine powder of antimony used as eye paint, and only later came to mean the distilled spirit. Elixir is al-iksir, an Arabic rendering of a Greek word for a drying powder. Athanor, the self-feeding furnace, is al-tannur, an oven. Even "alchemy" itself carries its Arabic article, and so does "azoth," the name Paracelsians would later give to the universal solvent.
