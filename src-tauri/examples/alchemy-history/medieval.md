Latin Europe received alchemy from Arabic sources in the twelfth century, argued about it in the universities of the thirteenth, and by 1300 had produced original work of its own, along with the first laws against it.

## Arrival

The first Latin alchemical text with a firm date is the Book of the Composition of Alchemy, translated by Robert of Chester in 1144 from an Arabic dialogue between the monk Morienus and the prince Khalid. Robert's preface explains what the word alchemy means, assuming his readers had never met it. Over the next decades translators in Spain and Sicily, Gerard of Cremona among them, turned out Latin versions of Jabirian treatises, of works attributed to al-Razi, and of the Turba Philosophorum, an Arabic assembly of ancient philosophers debating the nature of matter that was itself based on Greek sources. The Emerald Tablet arrived in the same wave.

What Europe got was a package: recipes, the sulfur-mercury theory of metals, Ibn Sina's argument that metals could not change species, and a habit of allegorical writing. It also got a reputation. The new science came with the new Aristotle and the new medicine, and it was taken seriously.

## Albertus Magnus and Roger Bacon

Albertus Magnus, the Dominican teacher of Thomas Aquinas, wrote a Book of Minerals around 1250 that discusses alchemy at length. He accepted that metals were formed from sulfur and mercury, and he reported visiting workshops and questioning practitioners. On transmutation he was cautious: alchemists could imitate the color and weight of gold, but the gold he had seen tested did not behave like natural gold under repeated firing. A large number of alchemical treatises were later attributed to him, none of them genuine.

Roger Bacon, a Franciscan writing in the 1260s, was more enthusiastic. In the works he sent to Pope Clement IV in 1267 he distinguished a speculative alchemy that explains how things are generated from the elements from a practical alchemy that makes things, and he argued that the practical art was more useful than any other science because it could produce medicines and prolong life. Alchemy for Bacon belonged with optics and mathematics as experimental knowledge, and his reputation as a magician grew from the same writings.

## The Summa perfectionis

The most important original Latin work of the period is the Summa perfectionis magisterii, the "Sum of Perfection," written in the late thirteenth century and circulated under the name Geber. Medieval readers took it for a translation of Jabir. It is not; there is no Arabic original, and the historian William Newman has identified its author as Paul of Taranto, a Franciscan teaching at Assisi.

The Summa is a lucid, well-organized textbook. It argues that mercury is the essential principle of metals and that sulfur is largely an impurity, a departure from the Arabic balance of the two. It explains why base metals are imperfect in terms of the fineness and arrangement of their particles, an early corpuscular theory of matter. And it describes sublimation, calcination, distillation, and testing methods in enough practical detail that a reader could follow them. Later works in the same tradition, written in the fourteenth century under the same name, contain the first clear European descriptions of mineral acids, including nitric acid and aqua regia, which dissolves gold.

## Medicine and the quintessence

By the early fourteenth century alchemy was converging with medicine. Distilled alcohol, "water of life," had been described in Latin recipe collections since the twelfth century, and physicians began to look on it as a preserved essence of wine. Around 1351 the Franciscan John of Rupescissa wrote a treatise on the quintessence, arguing that repeated distillation extracted from wine a fifth essence, incorruptible like the heavens, that could preserve the body from decay. He extended the idea to gold and to antimony, dissolved into medicines. Rupescissa was writing in prison for his apocalyptic prophecies, and his alchemy was meant to arm the church's poor for the coming tribulation.

The physicians Arnald of Villanova and, above all, the Majorcan philosopher Ramon Llull became the names under which enormous later corpora circulated. Llull, who died around 1316, wrote nothing on alchemy and disapproved of it; the pseudo-Lullian Testament, from the 1330s, and its many successors nonetheless made him the most cited alchemical authority of the fifteenth century.

## Suspicion and law

The art attracted regulation early. The Dominican order forbade its friars to practice alchemy in 1273, and other orders followed. In 1317 Pope John XXII issued the decretal Spondent quas non exhibent, "They promise what they do not deliver," which punished those who passed off alchemical gold and silver as real. It was directed at counterfeiting, not at the science, but it set a tone. In England, a statute of 1404 under Henry IV made the "multiplication" of gold and silver a felony; it stayed on the books until 1689.

The literary picture matched. Chaucer's Canon's Yeoman, in the 1390s, tells of an alchemist-priest who ruins himself and his servant chasing the stone while fleecing the credulous with sleight of hand. Fifteenth-century English verse treatises by George Ripley (1471) and Thomas Norton (1477) answer him in earnest, with Ripley's twelve "gates" of the work becoming a standard outline. Nicolas Flamel, a real Parisian scribe who died in 1418, was turned into a successful adept by a book published in 1612, two centuries after his death.
