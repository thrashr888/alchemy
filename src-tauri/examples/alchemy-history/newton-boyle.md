In the seventeenth century the two men most responsible for the new science of matter, Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton, were also serious alchemists, and the story of how alchemy became chemistry runs through their notebooks rather than around them.

## One word, not two

For most of the century there was no distinction between alchemy and chemistry. Writers used chymia, chemia, alchymia, and their vernacular equivalents interchangeably for a field that covered metallurgy, pharmacy, the analysis of minerals, the making of pigments and acids, and the attempt to transmute metals. Historians now use the old spelling "chymistry" to name that undivided subject. Chrysopoeia, gold-making, was one of its branches, respectable if secretive, and the same person could distill medicines in the morning and work on the stone in the afternoon.

## Boyle

Robert Boyle, born in 1627, published The Sceptical Chymist in 1661. The book demolishes two theories at once: the Aristotelian four elements and the Paracelsian three principles. Neither, Boyle argued, could be recovered from bodies by fire in the way their defenders claimed, and neither explained the results of actual analysis. In their place he offered a corpuscular view: matter is made of minute particles whose size, shape, and motion account for the properties of things. Modern chemistry treats the book as a founding text, though Boyle never proposed the modern definition of an element.

Boyle also believed in transmutation, and the corpuscular theory supported the belief. If gold and lead differ only in the arrangement of their particles, rearranging them is a mechanical problem, not a violation of nature. In 1676 he published in the Philosophical Transactions an account of a prepared mercury that grew hot when mixed with gold, which he took as a possible sign of action on the metal. He kept an alchemical correspondence, met with self-described adepts, and worked to have Parliament repeal the 1404 statute against multiplying gold, which it did in 1689. He had seen, he wrote elsewhere, things he could not explain.

## Starkey

The link between Boyle and the alchemical tradition was George Starkey, born in Bermuda in 1628, educated at Harvard, and settled in London from 1650. Starkey taught Boyle laboratory practice and supplied him with recipes. He also wrote, under the pseudonym Eirenaeus Philalethes and with a fictional biography attached, a set of treatises on the stone that became the most widely read alchemical works of the late seventeenth century. The Open Entrance to the Closed Palace of the King appeared in 1667, two years after Starkey died in the London plague. Its readers, Newton among them, took Philalethes for a mysterious adept. The identification with Starkey was established only in the 1990s.

## Newton

Isaac Newton left roughly a million words of alchemical manuscripts: transcriptions, indexes, laboratory notes, and drafts of his own treatises. He worked at a furnace in Trinity College, Cambridge, through the 1670s and 1680s, in the same years he was writing the Principia, and continued after moving to the Mint. His Index Chemicus cross-references hundreds of alchemical authors under thousands of headings. He translated the Emerald Tablet, annotated Philalethes closely, and pursued a "sophic mercury" that would open metals to reorganization.

The manuscripts were passed over when his scientific papers went to Cambridge and were sold at auction in 1936. John Maynard Keynes bought a large share, read them, and concluded that Newton was less the first scientist than the last of the magicians. Scholarship since Betty Jo Dobbs in the 1970s has argued that the alchemy was not a separate hobby: Newton's interest in active principles and in forces operating across distance fits his chemical work as well as his physics. Nearly all of the manuscripts are now transcribed and published online by the Chymistry of Isaac Newton project at Indiana University.

## The separation

The split into two words, one for the science and one for its discarded ancestor, happened in the eighteenth century. Herman Boerhaave, the most influential chemistry teacher in Europe, reported in the 1730s that he had heated mercury for fifteen years without finding any change, and treated the stone as a closed question. In 1722 Étienne-François Geoffroy read a paper to the Paris Academy cataloguing the tricks used to fake transmutation. By the time Lavoisier published his Elementary Treatise of Chemistry in 1789, defining an element as whatever analysis could not further decompose, "alchemy" had come to mean the pursuit of gold and the mystical writing around it, and "chemistry" everything else.

The change was not a discovery. No experiment showed that lead could not become gold, and in the twentieth century physics showed that it could. What ended alchemy was the loss of the theory that made it plausible, once chemists stopped thinking of metals as one substance at different stages of ripeness. The furnaces, the reagents, and the working habits were carried across the line unchanged.
