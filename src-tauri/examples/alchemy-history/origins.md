Alchemy as a written tradition begins in Roman Egypt, where metalworkers' recipes, Greek philosophy of matter, and religious allegory met in Alexandria between the first and fourth centuries CE.

## The name and the place

The word alchemy reaches English through Old French and medieval Latin from the Arabic al-kimiya, which in turn transliterates the Greek chemeia or chymeia. Where the Greek word came from is unsettled. One proposal ties it to an Egyptian name for the country, "the black land," so that the art would be "the Egyptian art"; another derives it from a Greek verb for pouring or casting metal. Both readings agree on the setting: the oldest surviving alchemical writing is Greek, and it was written in Egypt.

Alexandria under Roman rule had the ingredients. Egyptian temple workshops had centuries of experience with alloys, glass, dyes, and pigments. Greek natural philosophy supplied a theory: Aristotle's four elements, each convertible into the others, and his account in the Meteorology of metals forming underground from exhalations. Add the religious mixture of the period, with Egyptian, Greek, Jewish, and Gnostic ideas in circulation, and the result was a craft literature that increasingly explained its recipes in cosmic terms.

## The recipe books

The earliest layer is practical. Two papyri now in Leiden and Stockholm, written in Greek around the third or fourth century CE and probably found together at Thebes, contain more than two hundred short recipes: how to make an alloy look like gold, how to whiten copper so it passes for silver, how to tint stones to imitate gems, how to dye wool purple without real murex. Nothing in them promises true transmutation; they are shop manuals for convincing imitations.

A slightly earlier text, the Physika kai Mystika ("Natural and Secret Things"), circulated under the name of Democritus and is usually dated to the first century CE. Its author, sometimes identified with Bolos of Mendes, groups recipes under four headings: gold, silver, gems, and purple. Between the recipes he inserts the refrain that nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, and nature masters nature. The line is a theory in miniature. Substances have affinities, and the craftsman's job is to exploit them.

## Maria the Jewess

Among the earliest named practitioners is Maria, called "the Jewess" or "the Prophetess" in later sources. She is known only through quotations in Zosimos, which places her somewhere between the first and third centuries CE. What survives of her is technical. She is credited with the tribikos, a still with three copper delivery tubes; with the kerotakis, a closed vessel in which metal leaves were exposed to the vapors of sulfur, mercury, or arsenic; and with the gentle water bath that still carries her name in French and Italian kitchens as the bain-marie. Later alchemists also attributed to her a numerical maxim about one becoming two, two becoming three, and the third producing the one as a fourth.

## Zosimos of Panopolis

The first alchemist with a substantial surviving body of work is Zosimos, active around 300 CE and born in Panopolis, the modern Akhmim in Upper Egypt. He wrote in Greek, and Byzantine tradition credited him with a twenty-eight-volume encyclopedia of the art. What remains is fragmentary, preserved in medieval Greek compilations, the most important of which is a manuscript in Venice copied in the tenth or eleventh century.

Zosimos is a hinge figure. On one side he is a working chemist who describes furnaces, sublimation, distillation, and the treatment of metals with vapors, and who quotes and criticizes his predecessors by name. On the other side he writes dream visions in which a priest is dismembered, boiled, and transformed, and in which the stages of the work are staged as a ritual of death and rebirth. He also frames the art in religious terms borrowed from Hermetic and Gnostic writing: the soul is purified as metals are, and knowledge comes by revelation as much as by experiment. Nobody before him combined the two so fully, and both halves shaped everything that followed.

## Hermes and the Emerald Tablet

Hermes Trismegistus, a fusion of the Greek Hermes with the Egyptian Thoth, was already the patron of learned magic and philosophy in this period. The Corpus Hermeticum, a set of Greek philosophical dialogues from the second and third centuries, contains no chemistry at all. Alchemy's association with Hermes came later and stuck: the sealed flask became the "hermetic" vessel, and the art itself the "hermetic art."

The text most often attached to his name, the Emerald Tablet, does not survive in Greek. Its earliest known version is Arabic, embedded in a work on the secrets of creation attributed to Apollonius of Tyana and dated to around the eighth or ninth century. It reached Latin in the twelfth century and became the most quoted short text in Western alchemy, with its opening claim that what is below corresponds to what is above. Newton made his own English translation of it.

## What was passed on

By the seventh century the Greek tradition was being summarized in Byzantium, where Stephanus of Alexandria lectured on it under Emperor Heraclius. Those compilations, along with Syriac translations, are what Arabic scholars inherited. The recipes, the theory of a single underlying matter, the allegorical mode of writing, and the sense that the work of the furnace mirrored the work of the soul all arrived together.
