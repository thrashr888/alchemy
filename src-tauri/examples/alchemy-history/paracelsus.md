Paracelsus turned alchemy toward medicine in the early sixteenth century, replaced the two principles of metals with three, and started a school of chemical physicians whose quarrels reshaped European medicine over the next hundred years.

## The man

Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim was born in 1493 in Einsiedeln, Switzerland, the son of a physician, and died in Salzburg in September 1541. The name Paracelsus, which he used from the 1520s, is of uncertain origin. He learned mining and metallurgy in his youth, claimed a medical doctorate from Ferrara, served as a surgeon with armies in Italy and the Netherlands, and spent most of his life on the road. In 1527 he was briefly town physician and university lecturer in Basel, where he taught in German instead of Latin, threw a standard medical textbook on a St. John's Day bonfire, and antagonized the faculty so thoroughly that he left within the year.

Almost nothing he wrote was printed in his lifetime. The exception, his Great Surgery of 1536, was a success. The rest circulated in manuscript until Johannes Huser collected and published the medical and philosophical works in Basel between 1589 and 1591, half a century after his death. That delay matters: the Paracelsian movement was built by readers who never met him.

## Three principles

Arabic alchemy had explained metals as combinations of sulfur and mercury. Paracelsus added a third, salt, and applied the three to all bodies, not just metals. Sulfur was what burns, mercury what rises as vapor or flows, and salt what remains as ash and gives a thing its solidity. Burn wood and you see all three: the flame, the smoke, and the residue. The tria prima were not chemicals in a jar but principles, and every substance contained all of them in its own proportion.

For Paracelsus this was as much theology as chemistry. The world was created by separation, a divine act of chemistry that drew the elements out of a primal matter, and the physician's art repeated that act at small scale. He called the method spagyric, from Greek words for separating and combining. Alchemy was not primarily about gold at all; the cook, the baker, and the smith were alchemists, and so was the digestive organ he called the archeus, which separated nourishment from poison inside the body.

## Disease and remedy

Galenic medicine explained illness as an imbalance of the four humors and treated it with diet, bleeding, and compound herbal drugs. Paracelsus rejected the framework. Diseases were specific entities with external causes, seeds that lodged in a particular organ, and each required a specific remedy. He looked for those remedies in minerals: preparations of antimony, mercury, arsenic, iron, and lead, purified and dosed by chemical means. His Seven Defenses of 1538 answered the charge that these were poisons with the argument that everything is a poison and only the dose decides whether a thing is harmful, a line that toxicology still repeats in paraphrase.

He also drew on the doctrine of signatures, the idea that a plant or mineral's appearance indicates its use, and on astrology, holding that each organ corresponded to a planet. He treated miners' lung disease as an occupational illness, one of the first writers to do so, and wrote on syphilis, on the plague, and on mental illness with a mix of observation and cosmology that reads unlike anyone before him.

## The Paracelsians

The chemical physicians who took up his program in the later sixteenth century were called iatrochemists, from the Greek for physician. Petrus Severinus in Denmark systematized the doctrine in 1571 into a form that university-trained readers could use. Joseph Du Chesne in France and Oswald Croll in Germany published recipe books; Croll's Basilica chymica of 1609 became a standard pharmacopoeia of mineral remedies. The Paris medical faculty banned antimony in 1566 and fought the Paracelsians for a century before the ban was lifted in 1666, by which time antimony compounds were in general use.

The most original of the heirs was Jan Baptist van Helmont, a Flemish nobleman who lived from 1580 to 1644. He took over the archeus and the search for specific remedies but discarded the three principles along with the four elements, holding that water was the single material of all things. To show it he grew a willow in a weighed tub of soil for five years, watered it, and found the tree had gained over 160 pounds while the soil had lost only two ounces. He coined the word gas for the substances given off by burning charcoal and fermenting grapes that were not air. He also reported, with evident conviction, having transmuted mercury into gold with a grain of powder given to him by a stranger. His collected works, the Ortus medicinae, appeared in 1648 and shaped chemistry in England and the Netherlands for the rest of the century.

## What changed

Paracelsus did not end the search for gold. He shifted the center of gravity. After him the leading use of alchemical technique was pharmacy, the chemical laboratory became a fixture of medicine, and a physician could describe himself as a chemist without apology. The word "chemistry" in its modern sense grew out of that shift, and so did much of the eighteenth-century opposition to alchemy, which was in part a reaction against the Paracelsians' cosmology once their drugs had been absorbed.
