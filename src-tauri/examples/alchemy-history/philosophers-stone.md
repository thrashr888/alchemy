The philosopher's stone was the imagined substance that could turn base metals into gold, and for most of alchemy's history it was the goal that organized the work.

## The idea

The stone rests on a theory of metals shared by Greek, Arabic, and Latin alchemy: metals are not fundamentally different substances but one kind of thing at different stages of purity. In the earth they ripen slowly toward gold. The art can do quickly what nature does over ages, provided it can find the agent that drives the ripening. That agent is the stone, also called the elixir, the tincture, the medicine of metals, or simply the red powder.

The mechanism was projection. A small quantity of the stone, wrapped in wax or paper, was thrown onto a much larger mass of molten lead, tin, or mercury, and the whole mass was said to convert. Accounts disagree on the ratio, from one part in ten to one part in many thousands. The same substance, taken internally in tiny doses, was held to cure disease and extend life; the stone was a universal medicine as well as a universal metal.

## The work

The stone had to be made, and the process of making it was the Great Work, the magnum opus. Texts describe it as a sequence of color changes in the sealed vessel: a blackening (nigredo) as the starting matter putrefies, a whitening (albedo) that yields the lesser stone capable of making silver, sometimes a yellowing (citrinitas), and a final reddening (rubedo) that yields the stone proper. Between the black and the white many writers place a display of shifting colors called the peacock's tail.

What went into the vessel was the real secret and the point on which every text is evasive. The first matter, the prima materia, was described under hundreds of cover names. Some authors meant ordinary mercury, some a "philosophical" mercury prepared from metals, some antimony ore, some a substance drawn from dew, urine, or vitriol. Fifteenth-century English writers such as George Ripley reduced the work to a set of named operations: calcination, dissolution, separation, conjunction, putrefaction, congelation, cibation, sublimation, fermentation, exaltation, multiplication, and projection. Later readers could follow the outline while still lacking the one fact that mattered.

## Fraud and the assay

Because gold was money, claims to make it were policed. Assayers tested by touchstone, by weight against volume, and by cupellation, in which the sample is melted with lead in a porous cup so that base metals oxidize into the cup and only gold and silver remain. Alchemical gold that failed cupellation, or that would not survive repeated melting, was exposed. Fraud was common enough to have a literature. Chaucer's Canon's Yeoman describes a crucible with a hollow charcoal lid hiding a lump of silver, and a hollow stirring rod plugged with wax. English law made "multiplying" a felony in 1404; Pope John XXII's decretal of 1317 targeted the counterfeiters rather than the art.

## The adepts

The men said to have possessed the stone form a distinct genre of story. Nicolas Flamel, a Paris scribe who died in 1418, became an adept only in a book of 1612 that told how he had deciphered a mysterious manuscript and made gold in 1382. Edward Kelley, working with the mathematician John Dee, claimed to make gold with a powder found in a ruined abbey; he performed for Emperor Rudolf II in Prague, was knighted, and died in 1597 after imprisonment when he could not repeat the trick. Michael Sendivogius, a Polish alchemist at the same court, published a New Light of Chemistry in 1604 that stayed in print for a century.

In 1667 the Dutch physician Johann Friedrich Schweitzer, writing as Helvetius, published an account of a stranger who visited him in The Hague, gave him a fragment of the stone, and departed; Helvetius reported that he had transmuted lead with it and had the gold assayed. Johann Friedrich Böttger, an apothecary's apprentice who let it be known around 1701 that he could make gold, was seized by Augustus the Strong of Saxony and held for years to produce it. He never did, but his forced research yielded Europe's first hard-paste porcelain, and the Meissen factory opened in 1710.

## Believers among the founders of chemistry

Belief in the stone did not stop at the scientific revolution. Robert Boyle published a paper in 1676 on a specially prepared mercury that grew hot when mixed with gold, which he took as a sign it might be working on the metal, and he lobbied for the 1689 repeal of the English statute against multiplying. Newton spent decades on the problem. The stone did not fade because it was refuted; it faded in the eighteenth century because chemists stopped finding the underlying theory of metals useful.

## Afterward

Transmutation of elements turned out to be real, though not by the furnace. Ernest Rutherford converted nitrogen to oxygen in 1919 by bombarding it with alpha particles. In 1941 physicists at Harvard produced gold isotopes by irradiating mercury with neutrons, and in 1980 Glenn Seaborg's group made trace gold from bismuth in a particle accelerator. In every case the cost exceeds the value of the gold by many orders of magnitude, which the alchemists would have found a familiar problem.
