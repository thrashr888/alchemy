GE Aerospace (GE), headquartered in Evendale, Ohio, is what remains of General Electric after the historic three-way split completed in April 2024 (GE HealthCare and GE Vernova having been spun off): a pure-play jet engine company and, by installed base, the largest in the world. Through CFM International, its joint venture with Safran, it powers most narrowbody aircraft (LEAP engines for the Boeing 737 MAX and Airbus A320neo), alongside widebody engines (GE9X, GEnx) and a large defense business. In fiscal 2024, its first year standalone, GE Aerospace reported revenue of about $39 billion and net income of roughly $6.6 billion.

The economics rest on the razor-and-blades aftermarket: engines sell near cost, and decades of spare parts and shop visits generate the profit — services are roughly 70 percent of revenue, and the fleet of tens of thousands of engines keeps aging favorably. Strategic themes: strained supply chains limiting LEAP output while airlines fly older engines longer (good for aftermarket, awkward for deliveries); durability fixes for LEAP hot-section parts to lift time-on-wing; the GE9X ramp for the delayed Boeing 777X; defense growth amid rising military budgets; development of the RISE open-fan demonstrator for next-generation fuel efficiency; and heavy capital returns as CEO Larry Culp's turnaround — from near-collapse conglomerate to focused aerospace champion — became a management-classic case study.
